Economics as a Human Science

In the human sciences, specifically economics, we study how humans allocate scarce resources. Every dollar spent on one problem (like climate change) is a dollar that cannot be spent on another (like malaria or education). This is called Opportunity Cost.

Economists use Cost-Benefit Analysis to determine where money does the most "good." However, defining what counts as "good" is a Value Judgment, which is a core theme in Theory of Knowledge (TOK).

🔑 Key TOK/Economics Terms

  • Scarcity: The basic economic problem—unlimited wants vs. limited resources.
  • Opportunity Cost: The loss of other alternatives when one alternative is chosen.
  • Normative Statement: A value-based claim about what "should" be done (subjective).
  • Positive Statement: A fact-based claim about what "is" (objective).
  • Effective Altruism: Using evidence and reasoning to determine how to benefit others as much as possible.

Lomborg: 2014 vs. 2023 ("Best Things First")

The video hook is from 2014. In 2023, Lomborg released his newest research, "Best Things First," which identifies the 12 most efficient policies for the world.

(a) Best Things First (2023 Update)

Lomborg now argues that for just $35 billion a year, we could save 4.2 million lives annually by focusing on 12 specific areas (e.g., tuberculosis treatment, childhood immunization, and malaria).

Question:

Has the core argument changed? Is money still being directed to "big projects" (large CO2 mitigation) over these "effective projects"? Explain your view.

(b) The "Green Innovation" Shift

In 2014, Lomborg said green energy wasn't ready. Since then, the cost of solar and wind has dropped by ~90%. However, storage (batteries) remains expensive.

Question:

If the data shows green technology is now cheaper, does this change the economic "Cost-Benefit" calculation for climate spending compared to 8 years ago?

(c) Effective Altruism: GiveWell

GiveWell uses data to find the most cost-effective charities. They found that providing anti-malaria bed nets ($5 per net) is orders of magnitude more "efficient" at saving lives than most domestic policies in wealthy countries.

Question:

If we use "lives saved per dollar" as our only metric, does it become difficult to justify expensive climate projects? What other metrics (e.g., future generations) might we be missing?

Data-Driven Giving: Giving Multiplier

Giving Multiplier is a platform that uses behavioral economics to encourage people to donate to "Super Effective" charities (like those recommended by GiveWell) by matching donations.
