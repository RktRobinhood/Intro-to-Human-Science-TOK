🔑 Essential TOK Terms

  • Validity: Does the experiment actually measure what it says it's measuring? (e.g., measuring 'intelligence' vs. measuring 'test-taking skill').
  • Reliability: If we repeat the study exactly, will we get the same results?
  • Observer Effect: The phenomenon where the act of observing a human behavior changes that behavior.
  • Leading Questions: Questions that suggest a particular answer or contain information the subject did not previously have.
  • Self-Fulfilling Prophecy: When a belief or expectation leads to its own fulfillment.

Developing a TOK Argument

💡 Pedagogical Tip: A strong TOK argument doesn't just say "bias is bad." It explores how bias limits knowledge and why we might still value that knowledge anyway.

Step 1: The Thesis Statement

Create a 'To what extent' claim. Example Starter: "To what extent can we trust Human Science findings if the method of data collection is inherently subjective?"

Step 2: Scrutinizing the Evidence

Look at the studies you analyzed (Loftus, Hans, etc.). Question: Are these studies just 'exceptions,' or do they represent a fundamental flaw in how we study humans? How robust is this evidence?

Step 3: The Counterclaim

Challenge your own thesis. Starter: "However, proponents of Human Science might argue that through rigorous controls (like double-blind trials) and peer review, we can achieve a level of objectivity that..."

Step 4: The Evaluation (The 'So What?')

Weigh the two sides. Given the problems of expectation and belief, should we still use Human Science to make policy or law? What is the final verdict?
