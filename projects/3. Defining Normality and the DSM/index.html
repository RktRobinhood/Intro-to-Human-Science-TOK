What You Should Know

In psychology and psychiatry, the DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) is a major classification system used to describe and diagnose patterns of thoughts, emotions, and behaviours that are considered mental disorders. Importantly, classification is not a neutral “labeling machine” — it is a human tool designed to improve communication, research, and treatment decisions.

Defining normal versus disordered behaviour is complicated because definitions depend on: statistical rarity, cultural norms, distress, dysfunction, and professional interpretation. That means boundaries can shift over time as evidence improves, social values change, and new perspectives emerge.

🔑 Key TOK Terms

  • Definition: A rule for membership in a category (what counts, and what does not).
  • Classification: Sorting people/behaviours into categories to support explanation and decision-making.
  • Normality: A benchmark of typical behaviour (often statistical, cultural, or functional).
  • Disorder: A pattern associated with clinically significant distress and/or impairment (often judged contextually).
  • Interpretation: How experts apply criteria to real cases; this introduces judgment, even with rules.
  • Knowledge claim: A claim presented as true based on evidence and reasoning.

Case Study: The Bell Curve and Problematic Labeling

While the DSM attempts to classify behaviour for clinical help, other classification systems in the human sciences have been used to rank and marginalize. The 1994 book The Bell Curve by Herrnstein and Murray is a primary example of how labels (like IQ scores) can be used to construct knowledge claims that have significant social consequences.

Benefits vs. Risks of Normalizing

In TOK, we evaluate benefits (e.g., DSM labels that lead to better treatment and shared clinical language) against risks (e.g., *The Bell Curve* using labels like IQ to justify social stratification). When a human trait is mapped onto a "Bell Curve," the statistical definition of normality can be used to label entire groups as "naturally" less successful, often ignoring environmental variables like socioeconomic status or bias in testing.
