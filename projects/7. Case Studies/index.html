What They Tell Us — and What They Don’t

1) What Is a Case Study?

A case study is a detailed, in-depth investigation of a specific person, group, organization, event, or phenomenon in its real-world context. Case studies typically draw on multiple sources (e.g., interviews, observations, documents, and sometimes quantitative data) to build a rich, contextualised picture of the case.

🔑 Key TOK / Human Sciences Terms

  • Context: The social/cultural/situational conditions shaping behaviour and meaning.
  • Generalizability: How confidently findings apply beyond the specific case.
  • Evidence: Data and observations used to support a claim (often qualitative in case studies).
  • Interpretation: How researchers make sense of evidence; can introduce subjectivity.
  • Trustworthiness: How credible findings are (transparency, triangulation, and alternative explanations matter).

2) Why Human Scientists Use Case Studies

Common reasons

  • Complex, real-world phenomena: behaviour often cannot be meaningfully separated from context.
  • Rich, detailed data: deep evidence from multiple sources can explain how and why things happen.
  • New concepts and questions: cases can reveal unexpected patterns that inform theory development.
  • Rare or unique situations: some phenomena are too uncommon for large-scale studies.

3) What Case Studies Do Well

  • Mechanisms and processes: helps trace what happened and how it unfolded.
  • Context preservation: keeps cultural/social/organizational setting visible.
  • Theory refinement: can challenge theories or show where they don’t fit.

4) What Case Studies Cannot Do

  • Limited generalisability: one case ≠ a whole population.
  • Bias risk: researcher interpretation and selection can shape conclusions.
  • Causation limits: often shows patterns/relationships, but rarely proves cause by itself.
  • Time/resources: deep data collection is demanding compared with surveys.

5) Famous Case Study Examples

These examples show how studying a single case can produce powerful insights — and also why we must be careful about generalising.

(a) H.M. (Henry Molaison) — Memory

After brain surgery for epilepsy, H.M. lost the ability to form new long-term explicit memories. Researchers learned that different memory systems rely on different brain structures, reshaping theories of memory.

Question

What did H.M.’s case reveal about memory, and why would it be hard to learn this from a survey?

(b) Phineas Gage — Brain and Personality

After a severe brain injury, Gage’s behaviour and personality reportedly changed. The case became a landmark in linking frontal-lobe function to personality and decision-making.

Question

Identify one strength and one limitation of using Phineas Gage as evidence for brain–personality links.

(c) Genie — Language Acquisition

Genie experienced extreme social isolation and limited language exposure until adolescence. Her case informed debates about critical periods for language development, while raising serious ethical questions about research and care.

Question

How does Genie’s case show both the value and the ethical complexity of case studies?

(d) Business / Management Case Studies

Business schools commonly use case studies of organizations, crises, or strategic decisions to practise analysis and decision-making in realistic contexts. This is often teaching-focused, but it also shows how theory meets messy real-world constraints.

Question

Give an example of a business case study topic (e.g., a company’s crisis response). What sources of evidence would be useful?

6) TOK Connections — Knowledge in the Human Sciences

Depth vs. Breadth

Case studies often trade breadth (generalization to populations) for depth (contextual understanding). We may know a great deal about one case, but be less certain about how common the pattern is.

Evidence and Interpretation

Evidence is frequently qualitative and narrative. This can be credible, but it depends on careful interpretation, transparency about methods, and considering alternative explanations.

Theory Building vs. Theory Testing

Case studies often help generate hypotheses and concepts. Other methods (e.g., experiments, surveys) may later test how widely the ideas apply.

7) Classroom Activities

Activity 1 — Compare Methods

Pick a phenomenon (e.g., language learning). Write three research questions: one best answered by a case study, one by a survey, and one by an experiment. Explain why each method fits.

Activity 2 — Evaluate a Famous Case

Choose one (H.M., Phineas Gage, or Genie). Summarise what it taught us, identify at least one limitation, and reflect on how much certainty we can claim from this case.
