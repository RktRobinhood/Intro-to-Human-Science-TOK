🔬 The Replication Crisis

Exploring Reliability and Statistics in Human Sciences

📺 Watch First: Why Science Has a Replication Problem

What You Should Know

In the human sciences, researchers collect data about behaviour, thought, and society. Because these areas are complex, human scientists rely heavily on statistical analysis to decide whether findings are significant or likely due to chance. However, when many results cannot be repeated by other researchers, we call this the replication crisis — a problem with reliability of knowledge in science.

Replication means repeating a study using the same methods to see if the same findings occur again. Replication gives confidence that a finding is real and not just a statistical fluke.

🔑 Key TOK Terms

  • Replication: Repeating a study to see if the same result occurs.
  • Statistical significance: A result that is unlikely (below a chosen threshold like p < .05) to have happened by chance.
  • Validity: Whether a study measures what it claims to measure.
  • Reliability: Whether the same results can be obtained again under similar conditions.
  • Knowledge claim: A statement presented as fact based on evidence.

Case Studies & Examples

These examples show how apparently important results didn't hold up when others tried to replicate them.

(a) The Reproducibility Project — 100 Psychology Studies

The Reproducibility Project attempted to replicate 100 influential psychology experiments. Only about 36% of those studies produced results similar to the originals, meaning most findings could not be reliably repeated — and even the replicated effects tended to be weaker.

(b) Ganzfeld ESP Experiments

In the 1980s and 1990s, psychologists conducted experiments using the ganzfeld technique to test extrasensory perception (ESP). Initial studies claimed statistically significant evidence for psi phenomena. However, later, larger replication attempts failed to confirm these results.

Example Study: "Does psi exist? Lack of replication of an anomalous process of information transfer" (Milton & Wiseman, Psychological Bulletin)

(c) Publication Bias and Questionable Practices

A major review on the replication crisis highlights how questionable research practices such as p-hacking (trying many statistical tests until one looks significant) and HARKing (forming hypotheses after seeing the results) distort what appears in published science.

(d) Simpson's Paradox

Simpson's Paradox occurs when a trend seen in several groups reverses when the groups are combined. This shows how statistical interpretation without understanding context can mislead.
